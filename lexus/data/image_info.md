1. Transparent cellulose-aerogel “window” demo — shows how a cellulose aerogel sheet can be optically clear yet block heat, originally explored for energy-saving glazing. Think of this as a thin, light, insulating insert.
https://www.glassonweb.com/article/highly-transparent-silanized-cellulose-aerogels-boosting-energy-efficiency-glazing


2. Nanoscale network of a cellulose aerogel — TEM images reveal a web of nanofibres with sub-100 nm pores; that ultra-porous structure is why it insulates thermally and can absorb sound.
https://www.nature.com/articles/s41560-023-01226-7



3. How freeze-drying changes structure — side-by-side photos and SEMs of cellulose aerogels made with different freezing rates. Faster freezing preserves a finer, more uniform pore network (useful for tuning acoustic/thermal behavior).
https://jwoodscience.springeropen.com/articles/10.1007/s10086-015-1514-7


4. Bacterial cellulose, from gel to aerogel — lab photos of a cultured BC film and the resulting aerogel pieces. This is the “grown, then dried” route we’d use for bio-made liner cores.
https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/bioengineering-and-biotechnology/articles/10.3389/fbioe.2020.603407/full

Bio-Grown Aerogel Cabin Liner: a thin, lightweight layer grown from cellulose/bacterial cellulose and laminated behind standard interior skins. Its nanoporous network blocks heat and scatters sound, delivering quieter, better-insulated cabins at lower weight. Made via low-temperature, water-based steps (grow → dry → treat → laminate) that fit current trim workflows.
